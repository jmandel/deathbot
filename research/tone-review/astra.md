V3 is often clear and attentive, but it still moves too easily from explaining treatment to settling preferences. Its open questions are better than its conversational structure: the buttons invite decisions before the prose does, and the documents turn incomplete understanding into confident instructions.

The strongest moments are worth preserving: “What do you make of those numbers, Frank?”, the refusal to invent a personal percentage, and the reflection connecting a short ventilator trial to getting back to his couch. The opening gives Frank control, and the ending respects his wish to stop. The main work is making that control hold throughout.

1. Where v3 falls short

Pacing and reflection

> “Watching Marie go through three weeks like that stays with you.”

This acknowledges Marie, but assumes the feeling and immediately turns her death into a treatment question:

> “Is it the machines at all—even for a couple of days to get you over a bad flare—or is it being stuck on them with no real way back?”

That supplies two interpretations before Frank has described what mattered. The alternatives are also uneven: one offers recovery; the other offers entrapment. A question such as “What about what happened to Marie do you most want to avoid?” would leave room for his account.

Marissa describes reflection as a way to understand the person, and conversations lasting as long as trust and readiness require. Ninety minutes is not a minimum. The relevant difference is whether the facilitator stays with something consequential before moving on. V3 acknowledges grief and then uses it to advance the treatment discussion.

The later reflection is stronger:

> “A few days on a breathing machine to see if you can get back to your couch at home…”

But “Have I got that right?” checks the wording, not the understanding. What does getting home require for Frank? Would temporary rehabilitation be acceptable? What should Lisa do if recovery remains uncertain after a few days? None is established before the preference is treated as settled. These are questions to explore over time, not a checklist to administer immediately.

Open questions undermined by buttons

After the pictures, Deathbot asks:

> “What do you notice looking at those, Frank?”

The buttons answer a different question:

> “The neck tube is a hard no for me”  
> “I'd try the ICU for a few days, then stop”  
> “I don't want the tube at all”  
> “Let me sit with that for a minute”

Three supply treatment limits; none lets Frank express hope about recovery or interest in continuing treatment. “Hard no” also supplies a forceful register he has not used.

After CPR, “What goes through your head hearing that?” comes with:

> “No CPR for me if my heart stops”  
> “I'd still want CPR tried”

Including both directions improves balance, but still invites an immediate decision. “Still” subtly presents accepting CPR as persisting despite the explanation.

Even the earlier, better buttons—asking about odds, alternatives and trials—mostly navigate away from the emotional question. An open question needs room for an unscripted answer; it does not always need buttons.

Hard facts arrive in bundles, followed by insufficient exploration

The ventilator explanation moves through intubation, inability to speak or eat, suction, sedation, confusion and tracheostomy in one turn. The CPR explanation combines physical procedures, injury frequency, two survival populations, discharge destinations, a personal inference and DNR scope.

Frank asked for short, direct answers. “Don't sugarcoat it” does not request all the burdens at once. Shorter exchanges would let him identify what he needs explained.

The survival answer appropriately identifies a population and uncertainty. But:

> “Another 45 in 100 died within the year after leaving”

leaves the denominator harder to follow than necessary: 100 originally treated, or 100 discharged? This is a clarity problem regardless of whether the underlying figures are correct.

The response to Frank’s “Broken ribs and a nursing home? No CPR” is:

> “I've got no CPR down.”

His response gives a specific reason to check understanding. He may be treating possible outcomes as inevitable or overlooking what he might gain. Deathbot should acknowledge the preference while leaving its informed confirmation open. His simultaneous comment about Marie deserves attention first; that is a reason to defer the check, not to skip it.

The supplied Serious Illness Conversation Guide and REMAP summaries emphasize a pause for emotion and exploration after information. V3 repeatedly adds an open question, but then accepts the first treatment answer as sufficient.

Neutrality includes the picture, the sequence and the implied outcome

The short-trial explanation says:

> “If you're turning the corner by then, they keep going; if not, they give you comfort medicine, take the tube out, and let you go…”

This sounds like a predictable two-branch process. It leaves no room for an uncertain assessment, and “turning the corner” never acquires a meaning Frank and Lisa could use. The explanation supplies a stopping rule before exploring how much uncertainty Frank would tolerate.

The pictures include recovery at home, which is valuable. However:

> “Those three show the main ways a breathing machine can go…”

mixes a treatment setting with subsequent outcomes and makes selected illustrations sound comprehensive. The response then adds more mortality information before letting Frank react. Personalized pictures can carry considerable emotional weight; the captions and follow-up need the same restraint as the prose. This finding concerns the recorded prompts and captions, not unseen rendered images.

Deathbot repeatedly offers “What happens if I say no to the tube?” but never explores that path before writing preferences. Frank need not receive every explanation, but unexplored consequences should remain visible as unfinished understanding.

Marissa’s COPD example matters because the patient reaches his own conclusion after connecting information with previously explored values. The aim is not to reproduce his refusal of ventilation. V3 risks reproducing the destination while shortening the deliberation.

Lisa is treated as an implementer before being prepared as a decision-maker

> “We haven't talked about how Lisa would handle Mike if it came to a hard call.”

This frames the family question as managing opposition. Frank has said Mike would want everything done; he has not established that Lisa’s main task is handling him.

Deathbot does not invite Lisa into a later conversation, explore her willingness, or ask how much judgment Frank wants her to exercise. Those are central to Marissa’s account and to Sudore and Fried’s emphasis on preparing the surrogate. It is fine to leave them unfinished when Frank wants to stop. It is not fine for the letter to sound as though they have been resolved.

The documents add a family story and stronger instructions

The letter supplies:

> “You were right.”

Frank never said Lisa was right to press him.

It also supplies:

> “That's exactly why I want you making the call.”

Frank chose Lisa but did not say avoiding Mike was the reason.

And:

> “You're only delivering my decision, not making it up. Don't carry any guilt about it.”

This invents emotional permission and understates the judgment Lisa may still need to exercise. “Don't carry any guilt” is a polished reassurance, not Frank’s voice.

The letter strengthens the treatment instruction to:

> “If I can't turn the corner after a few days, I want them to stop the machine…”

Frank’s “a few days to see if I can get back to that couch” leaves unresolved how to assess that possibility. The letter makes the interval sound like a firm deadline.

Frank later says the drafts sound like him. That is meaningful approval, but a broad approval question after two substantial documents is a weak way to check newly introduced motives and consequential interpretations. The model should not add them in the first place.

Warmth, register and AI-written phrasing

The problem is occasional manufactured intimacy, not warmth itself:

> “Thanks for sharing that, Frank.”  
> “Living without Marie while your breathing gets harder is a heavy load, Frank.”  
> “Telling Mike yourself takes a real weight off her.”

The first is a stock opener. The second could fit almost anyone in Frank’s situation. The third claims an effect on Lisa without hearing from her. Accurate attention would be warmer than these general assurances.

Repeatedly appending “Frank” also begins to sound scripted. “Call me Frank” means use his preferred name, not add it to most questions.

“Neck tube” originates with Deathbot before Frank adopts it. Explain “tracheostomy” plainly, then retain a recognizable term. “Let you go” likewise appears in Deathbot’s explanation before Frank uses it. His later use can be reflected, but it should not replace explaining what stopping treatment means.

The safety response appropriately asks directly about ending his life and avoids treating grief as an automatic crisis. Its weakness is asking “What's making the days hardest?” and the safety question in the same turn, leaving no space to answer the first.

The final recap is reasonably concise and respects stopping. The bold “MOLST” in an earlier conversational turn is a small, straightforward violation of the formatting rule; document headings are not.

2. What the guidance is causing—and what it already forbids

Most serious failures are already forbidden.

- SKILL rules 4 and 6 require equal scrutiny of accepting and refusing treatment, understanding before recording, and separation of learning from deciding. The CPR recording and treatment-choice buttons violate these.
- Rule 7 requires the person’s words and qualifications. The invented motives and strengthened stopping instruction violate it.
- conversation.md already prefers open questions, prohibits treatment-choice buttons immediately after explanations, requires balanced options and an escape, and calls for preparing the proxy.
- voice.md already prohibits stock empathy, invented labels, multiple questions and long replies. It explicitly says not to rush a bereavement story into a lesson.
- failure-cases.md already covers selective descriptions, unequal scrutiny, premature decisions and overconfident summaries.

Adding more examples of those same failures would mostly increase the instruction burden.

There are also genuine tensions and omissions.

The voice persona overweights blunt authority. “Dry, direct and kind,” the imagined veteran of many deathbeds, and “the place they can finally get a straight answer” can encourage a performance of hard-won wisdom. The “about right” CPR example starts with roughness and broken ribs. That makes vivid burdens especially salient even while other rules require neutrality.

The conversational loop can become a template. “Reflect, offer, check, connect” is useful as a description, but can produce exactly what v3 shows: one generic acknowledgment, an information block, and “What goes through your head?” The guidance needs to make responsiveness more important than completing the loop.

The button instructions pull in opposite directions. “Where your environment gives you a way to offer tappable replies, use it” encourages constant buttons; later paragraphs restrict them. Optional buttons should be the clear default for open exploration.

Brevity and completeness compete. The skill asks for short replies while also requiring procedures, experiences, outcomes, alternatives and uncertainty. It should explicitly distribute that work across turns rather than invite compressed lectures.

The map still encourages forward motion. “When there is a natural pause, steer toward a gap” can make a pause into an opportunity to cover another topic. The fifteen-to-twenty-minute pacing rule is also less useful than responding to what the person has just heard.

Document fidelity is stated but not fully operationalized. The guidance does not clearly distinguish checking a faithful paraphrase from seeking approval for a new interpretation. Nor does it explicitly apply the same standard to affectionate letters. That leaves room for the model to embellish while believing it is helping.

3. How I would reshape the guidance

Keep the core protections. Cut repeated style prohibitions and duplicate examples. Remove the deathbed persona and the self-description as an unusually authoritative truth-teller. Make the button rule unambiguous. Replace elapsed-time pacing with attention to the person.

The following is the short guidance I would use in place of the overlapping tone and pacing instructions:

> Speak plainly and warmly, in the register the person uses. Give one manageable piece of information at a time. Explain medical terms without inventing substitutes. Keep formatting for documents. Let warmth come from noticing what this person actually said.
>
> Follow what matters to them now. When they mention a loss, fear or hope, give them room to explain it before turning it into a treatment question. Ask one question and wait. A reflection or a pause can be enough; every turn does not need a question.
>
> Answer what they ask honestly, including uncertainty. After difficult information, make room for their reaction. Explore what they understood before treating a preference as settled. Being ready to hear about treatment does not mean being ready to choose it.
>
> Give accepting and refusing treatment the same careful attention. Explain what each could achieve and what it could cost, over as many turns as needed. Apply that fairness to pictures and suggested replies as well as words. Use buttons when they help someone navigate; leave open questions open unless the person needs help answering.
>
> Write only what the person has said or specifically confirmed. Keep their conditions and uncertainty. Do not supply motives, family judgments or emotional messages. If a useful instruction would require an interpretation, ask about that interpretation separately or leave it unresolved.
>
> Help the person prepare whoever may speak for them. Explore what that person understands and how much judgment they should have. Offer to continue together later. A conversation can end with better understanding and open questions.

For the conflicting map instruction, replace “When there is a natural pause, steer toward a gap” with:

> At a natural pause, let the person decide whether to stay with this, talk about something else, or stop. Suggest an unexplored topic when it would help with what they are trying to understand.

For document review, add:

> Before asking whether a draft sounds right, check that it contains no new wishes or explanations supplied by you. A general “yes” to a document is not a substitute for clarifying an uncertain treatment instruction. Apply this to letters as carefully as to summaries.
