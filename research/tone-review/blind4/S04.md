One thing worth knowing is how the odds actually look for someone with COPD on home oxygen. And needing a breathing machine for more than a couple of weeks—let alone a couple of months—usually means moving the tube to a hole in the neck (a tracheostomy) and recovering in a long-term care facility or nursing home rather than a regular hospital room.

If you want a clearer sense of where you stand, you can ask your doctor to go over your lung function tests—some use a tool called the BODE index (lung tests, breathlessness, weight, and a six-minute walk) for a rough picture, though it still can't give a date.

**Deathbot:** TV makes CPR look a lot cleaner and more successful than it is in real life.

In real life, CPR means pushing hard enough on the chest to break ribs in more than half of people, electric shocks (which only work for certain heart rhythms—about 4 in 5 arrests don't start in one), and often putting a breathing tube down your throat on the spot. In the 2025 US registry of adults whose hearts stopped outside the hospital, about 9 out of 100 survived when it happened at home. Inside the hospital, in a large US registry (2000–2021), about 23 out of 100 adults left the hospital alive—and in a Medicare study of survivors 65 and older, about 40 out of 100 went home while about 55 out of 100 went to a nursing or rehab facility.

**Deathbot:** To make it official in New York takes two pieces:

First, to put Lisa in charge, fill out the New York Health Care Proxy (form 1430, at health.ny.gov/publications/1430.pdf). You sign and date it in front of two adult witnesses—no notary needed, and Lisa can't be one of the witnesses. There's a spot right on the form to write your feeding-tube wishes. Give Lisa a copy, keep the original where she can find it, and bring a copy to your doctor.

Second, to stop an ambulance crew from doing CPR at home, a Health Care Proxy by itself won't stop EMS. Ask your lung doctor or primary doctor to fill out a New York MOLST form (DOH-5003, usually printed on bright pink paper) with you—that's a medical order covering no-CPR and your breathing-tube limits that both EMS and hospitals must follow. Keep that pink form somewhere obvious at home, like on the fridge.
