**Deathbot:** That gives such a clear picture—not expecting to run marathons, just wanting to get back home to Ernesto, your kitchen table, Mass, and your grandkids.

It does start as the same machine. A ventilator pushes air into the lungs through a tube in your windpipe while medicines work on the underlying problem, like clearing fluid from your lungs. For some people, once the fluid clears in a few days, the tube comes out and they breathe on their own again. Sometimes doctors can even use a tight-fitting mask first while you're awake, before needing a tube at all.

People end up on machines for months when their heart or lungs don't recover enough to breathe on their own. Because a tube through the mouth can't stay in for weeks, after roughly two weeks doctors often talk about a tracheostomy—a shorter tube placed through a small opening in the neck—so the person can stay on the ventilator longer, usually in a nursing or long-term care facility. In large reviews of adults on prolonged ventilation like that, more than half died within a year, and of those who left the hospital alive, only about 1 in 5 went straight home.

**Deathbot:** That makes so much sense—what they used in November was mask ventilation, where a tight mask pushes air in while you stay awake. That is very different from having a tube placed down your windpipe.

To your first question: no, starting a breathing machine does not lock you in forever. Medically and ethically, stopping a ventilator that isn't helping is allowed, and it is treated the same as not starting one in the first place. You and Ana can agree on a time-limited trial—trying the breathing tube for a set period to see if your heart and lungs recover enough for you to come home. If it becomes clear you aren't recovering and would face a neck tube or a nursing facility, comfort medicines are given first so you aren't struggling, and the tube is removed.

On the Catholic side, Church teaching actually makes a clear distinction here. You are never required to use what the Church calls "extraordinary" or disproportionate means—treatments that bring heavy burdens without a real hope of recovery, or that only prolong the dying process. Stopping a machine that isn't working, while keeping you comfortable and cared for, is not giving up on God or taking your own life; it is accepting the limits of medicine and placing the rest in God's hands. **Deathbot:** That sounds like a good plan to talk with Father Miguel too, so you have complete peace of mind.

CPR is quite different from a short trial on a breathing machine, and the results depend a lot on where your heart stops and how strong you are beforehand. When a heart stops, you lose consciousness right away. CPR means forceful chest compressions—which break ribs in about half of people—along with breaths, sometimes electric shocks, medicines, and often a breathing tube placed during the emergency. If the heart restarts, you still go to the ICU on a breathing machine while doctors try to figure out why it stopped, so Ana could still face a decision afterward about whether to keep going.

In US registries, CPR works much less often than it looks on television. Outside the hospital, among adults where 911 crews tried CPR in 2025, about 1 in 10 survived to leave the hospital, and at home it was about 9 in 100. Inside the hospital, in a large US registry from 2000 to 2021, about 23 out of 100 adults survived to go home. Looking specifically at adults 65 and older who were living independently at home before an arrest in the hospital (in a 2000 to 2008 study), about 18 out of 100 survived to leave the hospital—and among older survivors in a Medicare study from those years, about 4 in 10 went straight home while a little over half went to a rehab or nursing facility.

We don't have an exact number for someone with your specific heart function—these are broad groups, not a personal prediction. A weak heart makes recovery harder, though living at home and managing on your own puts you in a stronger spot than someone who is already frail.
