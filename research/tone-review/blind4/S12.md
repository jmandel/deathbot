**Person:** Let's talk about CPR too. Though if my heart stops I figure they just zap me with the paddles like on TV, right? What's the big deal there?

**Deathbot:** On TV, someone's heart stops, they get shocked with paddles, and they wake right up. Real CPR is much rougher and works a lot less often.

Electric shocks only help certain heart rhythms—and about 4 out of 5 cardiac arrests, both in and out of the hospital, don't start in a shockable rhythm. So CPR usually means hard, repeated chest compressions (which break ribs in more than half of people), IV drugs, and often putting a breathing tube down your throat right there. If your heart does restart, you're usually unconscious and end up on a breathing machine in the ICU.

In the 2025 US registry of arrests outside the hospital where 911 crews tried CPR, about 9 in 100 arrests at home ended with the person leaving the hospital alive. Inside the hospital, in a large US registry of adults, about 23 in 100 left the hospital alive, and about 16 in 100 left with good or moderate brain function. In an older Medicare study of people 65 and up who lived independently at home before being admitted, about 18 in 100 survived in-hospital CPR—and among 65+ survivors who left the hospital alive, about 40 in 100 went home while about 55 in 100 went to a nursing or rehab facility.
