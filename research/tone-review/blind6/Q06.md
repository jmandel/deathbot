**Deathbot:** Let's start with CPR, and then we can look at breathing machines next if you want.

When a heart stops, the person loses consciousness right away. CPR is an emergency attempt to restart it using forceful chest compressions, breaths, often a breathing tube, and sometimes electric shocks. The compressions are hard enough that in studies, roughly half of people end up with broken ribs.

In a 2025 US registry of adults whose heart stopped outside the hospital and 911 crews tried CPR, about 10 or 11 out of 100 survived to leave the hospital. When a heart stops inside the hospital, a review of studies up to 2012 found about 15 out of 100 people in their 80s survived to leave—and in a 2000–2008 US study of survivors 65 and older, about 40 out of 100 went straight home while more than half needed a nursing or rehab facility afterward. Those are group averages, not a prediction for you personally, and serious illness like heart failure lowers the odds further, though not to zero.

**Deathbot:** A breathing machine (ventilator) pushes air into your lungs through a plastic tube in your mouth and windpipe while doctors treat the underlying problem; the machine buys time, it does not fix the heart itself. While the tube is in, you cannot talk or eat, you are given medicine to keep you sleepy or comfortable, and nurses suction mucus through the tube.

With heart failure fluid on the lungs, doctors can often try a tight-fitting mask (like BiPAP) first while you stay awake. In trials for heart-failure fluid on the lungs, mask ventilation appeared to lower hospital deaths compared with standard oxygen, though the evidence is low quality.

When a tube is needed, some people come off in hours or days. For older adults overall, a 1996–2003 Medicare study of people 65 and older put on a ventilator found about 73 out of 100 died within a year (including in the hospital). In a 2015 Canadian study of ICU patients 80 and older (not all on a ventilator), about 44 out of 100 died within a year, and about 26 out of 100 were alive and back to their usual physical function. If someone still cannot breathe on their own after a couple of weeks, doctors may bring up a tracheostomy (a tube in the front of the neck) for longer-term support—and a ventilator can also be stopped later if it isn't helping.
