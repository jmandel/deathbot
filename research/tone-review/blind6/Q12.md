Something worth knowing about heart failure: it tends to go up and down rather than straight down. Bad stretches get treated, people come home, and it's often genuinely hard to tell which flare-up is a serious one and which is the one to plan around. That's a general pattern for people with heart failure, not a prediction about you — and it's part of why Ana keeps asking.

**Deathbot:** Your instinct is right, and this is exactly where a paper matters. If you can't speak for yourself, Texas law turns first to your husband, then to your adult children. So with nothing signed, Ernesto is the one the doctors would ask — and if his memory makes that hard, they'd move down the list without knowing what you'd want.

A signed form changes that. You name the person you choose, a doctor certifies in writing that you can't make your own decisions, and your named person speaks for you. In Texas it's called a Medical Power of Attorney. The state gives it away free, and it needs two witnesses or a notary.

**Deathbot:** Ana wouldn't have to be there in person. Once the form is signed and a doctor certifies you can't make your own decisions, the decisions are conversations with the doctors — almost always by phone, at whatever hour they happen. What she'd need is a copy of the form on file at the hospital and a clear idea of what you want.

Being in Houston does shape it, though. Naming Ana means the doctors reach her by phone, sometimes in the middle of the night, and Tomás would be the one standing in the room. Some people name the person who's close by for that reason; others name the one who understands the medicine. Both are good reasons.

Two things worth thinking about: whether you'd want a backup named in case Ana can't be reached, and whether Ana could carry out "keep trying as long as there's a chance" if she'd choose differently for herself. She's seen a lot in that ICU. What's your sense of how she'd take that?

**Person:** Oh, naming Tomás as the backup makes a lot of sense to me. That way, if Ana is working a shift or can't answer the phone right away, Tomás is right here in San Antonio and can step in. They're very close, so I know they'd talk things over together anyway.

Is there anything else I should know about putting this Medical Power of Attorney together? Like where I'd get the form, or what to do with it once it's signed?

**Deathbot:** Good — Ana as your agent, Tomás as the backup, and you telling Ana plainly what you want so she isn't left guessing. That last part matters more than the paper.

On the form itself. It's the Texas Medical Power of Attorney, and the state gives it free — the Texas HHS advance directives page has it, and hospitals, doctor's offices and the Area Agency on Aging usually have copies too.

To make it valid you need either two witnesses or a notary. If you use witnesses, neither Ana nor Tomás can be one, since they're your agents and your relatives — so two friends from the parish, or a notary at the bank. Neither of them has to sign anything.

One thing I want to be straight with you about: the state form names one agent, and I'm not certain it has a spot to write in a backup. Look for that when you print it. If there isn't one, your doctor or the hospital's patient representative can tell you the best way to name Tomás — the other Texas form, the Directive to Physicians, can also name a person.

After it's signed: keep the original where you can find it fast, give copies to Ana, Tomás and your doctor, and bring one to your next appointment so it goes in your hospital record. Texas has no registry that holds these.

And what it doesn't do — it only takes effect when a doctor certifies in writing that you can't decide for yourself, and it means nothing to an ambulance crew at your house.
