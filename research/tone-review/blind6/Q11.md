**Deathbot:** A breathing machine—a ventilator—doesn't fix the heart or lungs itself; it buys time while doctors treat what caused the spell. If someone still can't breathe on their own after about a week or two, doctors usually talk about a tracheostomy—a shorter tube in the front of the neck—for longer support. **Person:** Let's talk about CPR too. I know my heart is weak, and if it stops, I would want them to try to bring me back—especially if there's a chance I could still make it to my granddaughter's quinceañera. Is CPR something that's different when you're 81, or is it pretty much the same?

**Deathbot:** The procedure itself is the same at any age: forceful chest compressions, breaths (often with a breathing tube), medicines, and electric shocks if the heart rhythm is one that responds to a shock. Because compressions are so forceful, studies show about half of people who receive CPR end up with broken ribs.

What changes at 81—and with a weak heart—are the odds of leaving the hospital and what recovery looks like. In a 2025 US registry of all adults, about 9 in 100 people who had CPR at home survived to leave the hospital. For arrests inside a hospital, pooled studies of people aged 80 to 89 found about 15 in 100 survived to leave the hospital. Among older survivors (65 and up in a 2000–2008 US study) who left the hospital alive, about 4 in 10 went straight home, while more than half went to a nursing or rehab facility.
