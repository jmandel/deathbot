**Deathbot:** Let's start with CPR. When a heart stops, CPR means forceful chest compressions—strong enough to break ribs in more than half of cases—along with breaths, drugs, often a breathing tube, and electric shocks if the heart rhythm is one a shock can fix.

In US registries, about 1 in 10 adults whose heart stops outside a hospital leave the hospital alive (2025 data), and about 2 in 10 do when it happens inside a hospital (2000–2021 data). Those are averages across all adults; the odds are lower with older age, frailty, or serious illness, and leaving the hospital doesn't always mean going back to life as before.

**Person:** Different. On TV they pound on your chest for ten seconds and you sit up asking for a cup of coffee. 1 in 10 is pretty lousy odds. What about the breathing machine?

**Deathbot:** TV definitely skips the part where a third of people don't make it out of the hospital even after a heart restarts.

A breathing machine (ventilator) is different from CPR—it doesn't restart anything, it just pushes air into your lungs through a plastic tube in your windpipe while doctors treat whatever made you unable to breathe. While the tube is in, you're usually sedated, you can't talk or eat by mouth, and food goes through a feeding tube or an IV.

Some people only need it for a few hours or days and recover. Across all ventilated adults in a 6-state US study (2005), about 35 in 100 died in the hospital and about 31 in 100 went straight home. For people who end up needing it long-term—more than two weeks, often with a tube placed in the neck called a tracheostomy—a large review of 124 studies found about 6 in 10 died within a year.

Have you ever been around someone on a ventilator, or is this mostly new territory?
