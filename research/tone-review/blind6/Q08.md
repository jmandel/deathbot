# Conversation Q08

**Person:** Hello! My name is Rosa. A friend from my parish sent me this link after we had a talk at church about planning ahead. I'm 81 and I have some heart trouble, and my daughter Ana—she's a nurse—has been asking me if I've thought about what I'd want if I get really sick. I've never written anything down, so I thought I'd see what this is all about.

**Deathbot:** Hello, Rosa. It sounds like this came at you from two directions at once — your parish and Ana. That's often how it goes.

Since Ana is a nurse, she probably has a clearer picture of the medical side than most people do. Can you tell me a bit about your heart trouble — what your doctors have told you it is, and how you've been doing with it lately?

**Person:** Well, I've had heart failure for about six years now. At my last visit, my cardiologist told me my heart is pumping at about a quarter of normal. 

It's been a little rough this past year—I've had three hospital stays because of fluid on my lungs. I take a lot of pills every day, my ankles swell up, and I get pretty short of breath if I try to go up the stairs. But I still try to get to Mass most days when I'm feeling up to it! I'm really hoping to stay well enough to be there for my granddaughter's quinceañera next spring.

**Deathbot:** Three stays in a year is a lot — on your body and on your patience, I imagine. And it says something good that you're still getting to Mass when you can.

Heart failure at that level tends to go up and down: the fluid builds, you get it treated, you're better for a while, then it comes back. It's often hard to know which flare-up is the one that won't turn around. That's exactly why people plan — not because anything is imminent, but because the decisions come up suddenly.

Has your cardiologist talked with you about what to expect as things go on, or asked what you'd want if you got much sicker than this?

**Person:** No, my cardiologist mostly just talks about my pills, watching my salt, and keeping the fluid off. We haven't really talked about what happens if things get much worse.

To be honest, I've always figured that if I get really sick, I want the doctors to do everything they can. Only God decides when it's time, you know? As long as there's a chance, I'd want them to try CPR or put me on a breathing machine if I needed it. I've still got a lot to be here for, especially my granddaughter's quinceañera.

**Deathbot:** Your faith comes through in that, and I don't want to argue you out of it. Wanting everything tried is a real choice, and it doesn't need defending here.

What I do want is for it to be a choice you're making with your eyes open. On television CPR looks like someone waking up and talking. In life it's hard pushing on the chest — ribs often break — and in one large study of Medicare patients over 65 who had CPR in the hospital, about 18 in 100 left the hospital alive. Of those who did, many went to a nursing home or rehab rather than straight home.

When you picture the doctors doing everything they can, what does that look like to you?

**Person:** Well, 18 out of 100 is certainly lower than I would have guessed. My daughter Ana has mentioned things like that from her ICU, about how hard it is on the body. 

When I picture them doing everything, I guess I picture them working hard to get me through a bad spell so I can wake up and come home to Ernesto and my family. I certainly wouldn't want to be stuck on a machine forever if there's no hope of waking up, but if there's a real chance I can get back to my life and see my granddaughter's quinceañera, I want them to try.

**Deathbot:** That picture — getting through a bad spell and coming home to Ernesto — is what most people mean, and it's not the same as being kept going indefinitely.

What you're describing has a name doctors use: a time-limited trial. You try, and beforehand you agree on a point to stop and look at how it's going. The hardest part is that the answer often isn't clear in the first day or two; after CPR, when someone stays unconscious, the team usually can't judge the likely outcome for a few days.

There's also a difference between a few days on a breathing machine while the heart recovers, and weeks with a tube through the neck into the windpipe — that's the "stuck on a machine" picture. Which of those two is more what you have in mind when you say a real chance?
*[shown: card]*

**Person:** Oh, definitely the few days while my heart recovers. I wouldn't want to be weeks and weeks with a tube in my neck if I'm not waking up. That's the kind of thing Ana talks about from her ICU, and it just sounds awful. 

But if it's a few days to see if I can pull through, yes, I'd still want them to try. I just want to make sure I'm giving myself every chance God gives me.

How does something like that even work, though? If I'm unconscious, who decides when those few days are up? Does the hospital need a paper for that, or does Ernesto just tell them? I worry a little about Ernesto—his memory isn't what it used to be, and I don't want to put too much on him if he's already scared.

**Deathbot:** In the hospital, the doctors judge the medical question — how it's actually going. What you would want is decided by whoever speaks for you, in conversation with them. So those few days get looked at together: they say what they're seeing, and your person says what you'd want done about it.

But for that person to be anyone other than Ernesto, you'd have to name them. In Texas, if you haven't named anyone, the law turns to your spouse first, then your adult children. So he'd be carrying it by default, whether or not anyone asked him.

The trial itself would just be written in your chart by the team. The paper that matters is the naming — Texas calls it a Medical Power of Attorney, and it only takes effect once your doctor certifies that you can't make your own decisions.

About Ernesto: you're allowed to name Ana. A lot of people name the child who stays steady in a hospital and let the spouse be the spouse. Do you think Ana would be willing to do that?

**Person:** Oh, I think Ana would definitely be willing. She's the one who's been asking me about it, and since she's an ICU nurse, she knows how all of that works. Honestly, it's a big relief to think Ernesto wouldn't have to carry that by himself—he's 83 and gets confused when things are stressful.

If I name Ana, what would she actually have to do? And do I need a lawyer to fill out that Medical Power of Attorney paper, or is it something we can just do at home?

**Deathbot:** What she'd do: she'd be the one the doctors turn to when you can't answer for yourself. They'd tell her what's happening and what the choices are, and she'd say what you would want. It wouldn't give her any say while you can still decide things for yourself, and it covers medical decisions only — not money or the house.

And no, you don't need a lawyer. Texas has a standard form, free from the state health agency, and you sign it in front of two witnesses or a notary. It's a kitchen-table job.

Would you like to work out what you'd tell Ana — the few days of trying, and the not wanting weeks on a machine — so that when the time comes she's carrying your words and not just her own judgement?

**Person:** Yes, I'd really like that. It's such a comfort to know I don't need a lawyer and can just do it at the kitchen table! 

I'd hate for Ana to feel like she's guessing what I want, or for my son Tomás to feel left out—he lives right here in town and helps us out so much. How should I put it into words for them so they both know I still want a fighting chance, just not to be stuck on a machine forever?

**Deathbot:** Here's a try, in your words rather than mine:

"I want you both to know how I've thought about this. If I get really sick, I want the doctors to work hard to pull me through — a few days on a machine while my heart recovers is fine with me. What I don't want is to be kept going for weeks and weeks with no hope of waking up. If there's a real chance I can come back to my life, I want them to try. I just don't want to be stuck on a machine forever."

Does that sound like you? Change anything you'd say differently — especially if "no hope of waking up" doesn't sit right as your own phrase.

And one thing still open: Tomás could be named as Ana's backup on the paper, so he's part of it rather than on the outside looking in. Would you want that?

**Person:** Oh, that really does sound like me. You put it so clearly! Reading it like that makes me feel a lot better about talking to them.

And yes, naming Tomás as Ana's backup is a wonderful idea. Since he's right here in San Antonio and Ana is in Houston, that makes a lot of sense, and I know he'll appreciate being included.
