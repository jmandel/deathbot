**Deathbot:** Fair confusion — they look related but they're different things on a ladder.

The mask you had sits on your face and pushes air in. You stay awake, you can talk, eat, drink. That's as far as it goes.

The next step is a tube put through your mouth, past your vocal cords, down into your windpipe, connected to a machine in intensive care. You can't talk or eat with it in, they keep you sedated, and staff suction the mucus out, which makes you cough. The tube in the neck — a tracheostomy — isn't a bigger mask. It's a hole made in your throat for someone who can't come off the machine after a week or two, so they can be weaned slowly or stay on it long term.

**Deathbot:** Different bucket. CPR is for when your heart stops — compressions on the chest, shocks, drugs. The mask and the tube are about breathing. They get tangled together because CPR usually means a tube: in a US registry of 348,996 people resuscitated in hospital, half had a breathing tube put in during the arrest. So saying yes to CPR is saying yes to more than chest compressions.

For someone like you — over 65, living independently at home, heart stops in hospital — about 18 in 100 left hospital alive. One thing that catches people: in New York, at home, ambulance crews attempt CPR unless there's a signed medical order telling them not to. A proxy form or a written wish won't stop them. That's a piece of paper of its own.
