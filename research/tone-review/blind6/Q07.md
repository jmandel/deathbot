**Deathbot:** Unlike the tight mask, where you stay awake, a breathing machine requires intubation: after medicine to make you sleepy, a clinician passes a plastic tube through your mouth into your windpipe and connects it to the ventilator in the ICU. While the tube is in, you cannot talk or eat by mouth, nurses suction mucus out through the tube, and people often need sedatives—which can also cause heavy confusion called delirium.

The machine does not fix the heart or lungs itself; it buys time while medicines try to get the fluid off. For some people, the tube comes out after a few days. When someone still cannot breathe on their own after a week or two, doctors often talk about a tracheostomy—a shorter tube placed through a hole in the front of the neck—which is likely the longer ICU path Ana sees.

**Deathbot:** I do not have figures specifically for heart failure on a breathing machine, and I do not know your exact age, so these numbers are from broader groups of older adults—and they are group averages from past studies (mostly 1996 to 2014), not a prediction for you personally.

- In a US Medicare study of adults 65 and older who needed a breathing machine in the hospital, about 73 out of 100 died within a year (including those who died in the hospital), and about 27 out of 100 were alive a year later.
- When ventilation goes past two weeks or requires a tracheostomy, recovery gets much harder: across 124 studies, about 59 out of 100 died within a year, and among those who left the hospital alive, only about 19 out of 100 went straight home.
