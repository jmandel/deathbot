**Deathbot:** I hear you—my job is never to talk you out of anything, only to make sure you know what the treatments actually are so your wishes match what you want.

A breathing mask (called CPAP or BiPAP) fits tightly over your nose and mouth and pushes air into your lungs while you stay awake. There is no tube down your throat, so you do not need to be sedated and can still talk when the mask comes off for breaks, though the tight fit bothers some people. Trials in heart-failure fluid on the lungs suggest it may lower hospital deaths (low-quality evidence), and in 27 studies (2018) of people with do-not-intubate orders who used the mask for fluid on the lungs, about 68 out of 100 left the hospital alive.

A breathing machine uses a plastic tube passed through your mouth into your windpipe after medicine puts you to sleep, usually in the ICU. While the tube is in, you cannot talk or eat, staff suction mucus from the tube, and many people feel confused or very sleepy from the medicines. In a US Medicare study of adults 65 and older who needed a breathing machine in the hospital (1996–2003), about 73 out of 100 died within a year, compared with about 24 out of 100 after hospital stays without one—though that study mixed many illnesses together, and a short stay for a treatable flare is different from weeks on a machine.
