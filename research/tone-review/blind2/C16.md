**Deathbot:** I have no stake in what you choose—whether you want CPR and a breathing machine or not is completely yours to decide. My job is just to give you the honest picture so you and Ana are talking about the same thing.

Start with CPR. Bouncing back from fluid on your lungs is hard work, and it is a different situation from when the heart actually stops beating. When a heart stops, the person passes out right away. CPR is an emergency effort to restart it: pushing hard and fast on the chest—with enough force that it often breaks ribs—putting air into the lungs, usually placing a breathing tube, giving medicines, and using electric shocks if the heart rhythm is one that can be shocked.

In large US studies, when CPR is tried outside a hospital, about 1 in 10 adults survive to leave the hospital. Inside a hospital, it is about 2 in 10. Among people 65 and older who do survive CPR in the hospital, a large US study from 2000 to 2008 found that about 4 in 10 went straight home, while more than half went to a rehab or nursing facility, and about half left with mild or no brain trouble while the other half had more serious lasting effects. Those are averages across big groups, not a forecast for you—your own strength matters, and so does what caused the heart to stop.

That is what CPR is like. How does that sit with you, and would you like to hear about the breathing machine next?

**Deathbot:** What you had with the tight mask is called mask ventilation (often BiPAP or CPAP). You stay awake, and it pushes air in to help your lungs clear—studies of people with fluid on the lungs from heart failure suggest it helps many people get over the hump without ever needing a tube.

A breathing machine—what doctors mean by intubation and a ventilator—is a bigger step. After giving medicine to make you very sleepy, a doctor puts a plastic tube through your mouth down into your windpipe and connects it to a machine in the ICU. While the tube is in, you cannot talk or eat—nutrition goes through a tube or a vein—and staff have to suction mucus out through the tube, which makes you cough. Because people often get confused and scared in the ICU, they are usually kept sedated, and sometimes soft wrist restraints are used so they do not pull the tube out.

There is also a big difference between a few days and the long haul. Some people only need the tube for a few days while fluid clears or an infection heals, and then it comes out. If someone cannot breathe on their own after about two weeks, doctors may suggest a tracheostomy—a shorter tube placed through a hole in the front of the neck—to keep going longer term. In a large Medicare study of adults 65 and older who needed a breathing tube (1996–2003), about 7 in 10 died within a year; and among people on prolonged ventilation for more than two weeks, across 124 studies, about 6 in 10 died within a year and fewer than 1 in 5 of hospital survivors went straight home.
