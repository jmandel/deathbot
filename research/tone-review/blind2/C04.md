**Deathbot:** That's a pretty accurate description of BiPAP—it pushes air hard enough to keep your lungs open without putting a tube down your throat, which is why they try it first in COPD flare-ups.

**Person:** Sure, tell me what the next step is. If a breathing machine would get me better and back home, I'd do it, even if it takes a couple of months.

**Deathbot:** If BiPAP isn't enough, the next step is intubation—going on a mechanical ventilator in the intensive care unit. They give you medicine to make you sleepy and put a plastic tube through your mouth into your windpipe, hooked to a machine that pushes air into your lungs while they treat the flare-up.

While the tube is in, you can't talk or eat, so food goes through a tube and staff suction mucus out of your airway. If someone can't come off it after a week or two, they usually move the tube to a small hole in the front of the neck—a tracheostomy—which is more comfortable for the long haul.

Would you like to hear what the studies show about how people on home oxygen for COPD tend to do if they end up on a ventilator?

**Person:** Wait a second, a hole in the neck? I thought a ventilator was just a bigger mask. So what are my odds if I end up on one of those?

**Deathbot:** Yeah, a lot of people picture a bigger mask—that's actually what BiPAP is. A ventilator means a tube down your windpipe, and if you need it past a couple of weeks, a tracheostomy through the neck.

No one can give you an exact personal number, because studies only show how groups of people did. The closest group to you is a study of nearly 4,800 Medicare patients on home oxygen—average age 74—who were put on a ventilator for a COPD flare-up in 2008. Out of 100 people in that group, about 23 died in the hospital, another 45 died within a year of leaving, and about 31 were still alive a year later.

Those numbers are from 2008, and they don't tell us how many of those survivors actually made it home or got back to their old life. How you'd do depends a lot on how strong you are day to day and what else is going on with your health.

**Deathbot:** To be clear, those numbers are for people who get sick enough to need a ventilator—not everyone on home oxygen today. Even so, it's a jarring thing to hear when you're picturing another ten years.

When people do leave the hospital after a ventilator—especially if they're weak or have a tracheostomy—they often can't go straight home. They go to a rehab center, a skilled nursing home, or a long-term care hospital to try to build strength or wean off the machine. In a large California study of people 65 and older who got a tracheostomy for breathing failure, most left the hospital alive, but only about 6 out of 100 of those survivors went straight home.

**Deathbot:** Yeah, a lot of people assume the one who's nearby automatically gets the say, and it doesn't work that way. Naming Lisa on a New York Health Care Proxy form fixes that—it's a short form, and you just need two adults to witness you sign it, no lawyer or notary needed.

**Deathbot:** TV makes it look a lot cleaner than it is. Most of the time when a heart stops, a shock doesn't even apply—only about 1 in 5 arrests start in a rhythm they can shock. Mostly, CPR means pushing hard on your chest over and over (hard enough that in studies, more than half of people end up with broken ribs), giving drugs, and very often putting a breathing tube down your throat on the spot. If your heart restarts, you don't just wake up—you usually end up in the ICU on a ventilator while they try to fix what caused it.

In a 2025 US registry of adults whose heart stopped outside the hospital, about 9 out of 100 people whose arrest happened at home survived to leave the hospital. Inside a hospital, in a large North American study of people 65 and older who were living independently at home, about 18 out of 100 survived to leave—and of the older survivors who made it out of the hospital, more than half went to a rehab or nursing facility rather than straight home. With severe COPD on home oxygen, the odds of getting back home in good shape are generally lower than for healthier folks your age.
