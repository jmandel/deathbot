**Deathbot:** When a person's heart suddenly stops, they lose consciousness right away, and without immediate treatment they die within minutes. CPR is the emergency effort to restart the heart: the team pushes hard and fast on the chest, gives medicines and electric shocks if the heart rhythm allows, and usually places a breathing tube connected to a ventilator. Pushing hard enough to move blood breaks ribs in about 55 out of 100 people (across 60 studies where injuries were checked).

How often it works in studies depends a lot on where the arrest happens and how the person's health was beforehand:
- Outside the hospital, across about 136,500 US adults of all ages where 911 crews tried CPR in 2025, about 11 out of 100 left the hospital alive—including about 8 out of 100 with good brain function or moderate disability—while about 89 out of 100 died during the arrest or in the hospital.
- Inside the hospital, across 29 pooled studies of patients aged 80 to 89 (published up to 2012), about 15 out of 100 left the hospital alive and about 85 out of 100 died in the hospital. In a North American registry from 2000 to 2008 of hospital patients 65 and older who were living independently at home like you are, about 18 out of 100 left the hospital alive.
- Among those hospital survivors 65 and older who did make it out of the hospital, about 48 out of 100 left with mild or no brain injury, and 40 out of 100 went straight home, while 55 out of 100 went to a rehab or nursing facility first.

Those numbers come from large historical groups and don't separate out people with a heart pumping at a quarter of normal, so they aren't a personal forecast for you. When CPR does restart the heart—which happens initially in about two thirds of hospital arrests—the person wakes up or stays asleep in the ICU on a breathing machine while doctors see how the heart and brain recover.
