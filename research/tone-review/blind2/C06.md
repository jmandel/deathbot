On CPR, TV has done a number on all of us. In a 2016 survey of 500 people in a California emergency room, more than half thought CPR worked over three-quarters of the time. Real life looks very different, for three main reasons:

1. **What it physically involves—including the breathing tube.** First, electric paddles only work on certain "shockable" heart rhythms, and about 81 out of 100 cardiac arrests (both in the 2025 US out-of-hospital registry and in US hospital registries) do not start in a shockable rhythm—especially when the heart stops because the lungs ran out of oxygen. Instead, CPR relies on someone pushing hard and fast on your chest to pump blood by hand; in a 2024 review of 74 studies (16,629 patients), that force broke ribs in about 55 out of 100 people and the breastbone in about 24 out of 100. Second, CPR and the breathing tube go hand in hand: in a US registry of 348,996 hospital CPR attempts (2000–2021), half had a breathing tube put down their throat during CPR and another 21 out of 100 were already on a ventilator. If they get your heart restarted, you don't wake up sitting in bed like on TV—you wake up in the ICU on a breathing machine.
2. **How often it works outside the hospital.** If your heart stops and no one does CPR (a Do-Not-Resuscitate, or DNR, order), you lose consciousness right away and die within minutes. When 911 crews do attempt CPR outside the hospital (136,516 US adults in the 2025 CARES registry), about 11 out of 100 survived to leave the hospital alive (and about 89 out of 100 died); when the arrest happened at home, about 9 out of 100 left the hospital alive.
3. **How often it works in the hospital for someone your age, and what life looks like after.** Across 29 pooled studies of hospital CPR (to 2012), for patients aged 70 to 79, about 19 out of 100 left the hospital alive and 81 out of 100 died (similar to the 18 out of 100 survival rate for people 65 and older who had been living independently at home in a 2000–2008 North American study of 26,329 patients).
