**Person:** Breathing machines

**Deathbot:** A breathing machine—a ventilator—pushes air in and out of the lungs when the body can't do enough of it itself. Sometimes it's used for a short stretch: a few days after pneumonia, surgery, or an emergency, then weaned off as you get stronger. Sometimes a tube goes through the mouth or nose and sits in the windpipe. If it's needed for weeks, it may be replaced by a tracheostomy—a small tube through the front of the neck.

If someone is too weak to keep breathing, the machine can keep the lungs working while the underlying problem heals—or it may reveal that the problem isn't going to heal. That's why it's not simply “use the machine” or “don't”: the stakes shift with what caused the breathing problem and how reversible it is.

There are also less invasive options, like BiPAP through a mask, and a time-limited trial with a planned point to reassess. Each has a different trade-off.

**Deathbot:** Yes. You can put that ahead of time in writing, and you can choose someone to speak for you if you can't.

In New York, the usual pieces are:

- A **Health Care Proxy**: names the person who speaks for you; it's signed before two adult witnesses.
- Written wishes with it, sometimes called a living will: NY has no statutory living-will form, but written instructions can carry weight, especially with a proxy.
- A **MOLST**: a portable medical order signed by you and a clinician after a real discussion; EMS and facilities can follow it. It can address life-sustaining treatments. It's generally for people with serious illness or advanced frailty.
- A separate nonhospital DNR order, or a MOLST with DNR/DNI, affects what EMS does outside the hospital.

One nuance: you can't reliably pre-program "stop after day 13" or "only if there's a tracheostomy." Life rarely announces the exact day. What you can do is state what counts as acceptable to you, what would tell you or your proxy that treatment isn't helping, and how much judgment your proxy should have.
