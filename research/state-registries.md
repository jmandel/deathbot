# State advance directive, POLST and EMS DNR registries

2026-11-14

This note covers the 50 states and the District of Columbia. For each one it asks three things: does a state advance directive registry exist that a resident can file in; what is the state's POLST-type program (POLST, MOLST, MOST, POST and others), and does it have an electronic registry; and what out-of-hospital or EMS do-not-resuscitate order exists, and is there a registry for it. Each jurisdiction was researched on its own from state agency pages, statutes, regulations and program sites. An independent checker then reopened the sources, corrected claims and recorded what it could not confirm. Several state sites (michigan.gov, mass.gov, tn.gov, health.ri.gov, health.ny.gov, some legislature sites) blocked automated fetches. Where that happened, the claim rests on search summaries or mirror hosts, and the per-state notes say so.

Main caveat: registries open, close, change operators and lose funding often. Of the registries found, at least four have closed or gone dark in the last few years, and two more are due to open in 2027. Treat this note as a snapshot, and recheck a state's entry before telling a person to rely on it.

## What we found

- 13 jurisdictions have an active advance directive registry that a resident can file in themselves: Arizona, California, District of Columbia, Idaho, Louisiana (living wills only), Maryland (a state-recognized private service), Montana, Nevada, North Carolina, North Dakota, Vermont, Virginia and West Virginia. Most are free. California and North Carolina charge $10 per filing, and Louisiana charges $20.
- 11 jurisdictions have an operating electronic registry that holds POLST-type orders. Six are dedicated POLST/MOST registries mainly used by clinicians: Delaware, District of Columbia, New Jersey, New York (closed to new enrollment since May 2025), Oregon and West Virginia. The other five are general registries that also accept a POLST: Arizona, Idaho, Nevada, North Dakota and Virginia. Oregon is the only one where submission is automatic unless the patient opts out. South Dakota also keeps a state Comfort One (EMS DNR) database.
- 21 jurisdictions have no advance directive registry and no POLST registry, either operating or planned: Alabama, Alaska, Arkansas, Florida, Georgia, Hawaii, Indiana, Iowa, Kansas, Maine, Minnesota, Mississippi, Nebraska, New Mexico, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, Wisconsin and Wyoming. Wisconsin does let a person file a health care power of attorney with the county Register in Probate for safekeeping.
- 12 more are unclear or pending: Colorado, Connecticut, Illinois, Kentucky, Massachusetts, Michigan, Missouri, New Hampshire, Oklahoma, Rhode Island, South Carolina and Utah. Most have a registry required or allowed by law that never launched or has lapsed. Illinois (POLST only, due by Jan 1, 2027) and Massachusetts (ePOLST, April 2027) have firm launch plans.
- Closures and lapses:
  - Washington's Health Care Declarations Registry stopped taking filings on July 1, 2011.
  - Louisiana's LaPOST eRegistry was disabled on June 29, 2024, after its vendor stopped service.
  - Michigan's Peace of Mind Registry website now redirects to an unrelated shopping site.
  - Rhode Island's Know My Health upload pilot no longer resolves.
  - Kentucky's provider-only Kentucky Health Information Exchange (KHIE) registry was dropped from the state HIE's 2027 readiness filing.
  - New York's eMOLST stopped enrolling new providers in May 2025.
  - California's pilot POLST eRegistry ended in 2020. A statewide replacement is still in development.

## The national picture

- There is no national government registry for advance directives or POLST forms. National POLST's [state program directory](https://polst.org/state-programs/) lists all 50 states and DC as members of the National POLST Collaborative, and says nearly all states are working to grow adoption. None of the National POLST pages opened for this note state a formal position on registries. Its [2021 recommendations for integrating POLST forms with EHRs](https://polst.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/2021.12.10-Recommendations-for-Integrating-POLST-forms-with-Electronic-Health-Records.pdf) "strongly encourage" electronic completion of POLST and say facilities "should utilize, when available, a statewide ePOLST system." They also ask that a copy be given to the patient. A state planning study ([Minnesota](https://www.health.state.mn.us/facilities/ehealth/polst/docs/120823recommendations.pdf)) notes that a POLST is valid whether or not it is in a registry.
- The closest thing to a multi-state registry is private: ADVault's MyDirectives and its A|D Vault Exchange. It has been an [eHealth Exchange participant since April 2020](https://ehealthexchange.org/advault-inc-and-ehealth-exchange-deliver-advance-care-plans-directly-within-electronic-health-records/), so participating providers can pull advance directives and POLSTs stored there into their EHRs. In December 2025, [Oregon POLST Registry forms became retrievable nationwide](https://business.starkvilledailynews.com/starkvilledailynews/article/bizwire-2025-12-2-oregon-polst-registry-now-accessible-nationwide-through-mydirectives-ad-vault-exchange-via-carequality-and-ehealth-exchange) through it, via Carequality and eHealth Exchange (press release). The [February 2026 eHealth Exchange participant call deck](https://ehealthexchange.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/02_eHealth-Exchange-All-Participant-Call_February.pdf) carries MyDirectives' claim that every state and DC can reach documents in the exchange. That shows the network can reach every state; it does not show that clinicians actually check it. MyDirectives also underpins state efforts:
  - DC (CRISP DC) and Maryland (MHCC-recognized, linked to CRISP).
  - The Massachusetts ePOLST registry due in April 2027.
  - Oklahoma's state page, which links residents only to MyDirectives.
  - A June 2025 proposal to Delaware.
  - Vynca, another vendor, hosts the Idaho, Delaware and South Carolina portals, and hosted Louisiana's until 2024.
- The U.S. Living Will Registry (renamed the U.S. Advance Care Plan Registry) was a private New Jersey registry founded in 1996. It still ran in 2021, per its [press releases](https://www.newswire.com/news/living-will-registry-encourages-advance-care-planning-with-steep-21355831). On 2026-11-14 its consumer domains no longer serve registry content: uslivingwillregistry.com fails with a hosting certificate mismatch, and usacpr.net shows a web-host placeholder page. Its platform is still named in state registries: Virginia's registry links to a uslivingwillregistry.com subdomain, Vermont's registry servers are described as managed by USACPR, and New Jersey's psychiatric advance directive registry names it. Treat it as closed to the public, with an unclear status as a back-end for those states.
- Interoperability standards exist but are early. HL7's [PACIO Advance Healthcare Directive Interoperability guide](https://hl7.org/fhir/us/pacio-adi/2025Sep/formal_specification.html) (STU 2 ballot, September 2025) covers creating, querying and verifying advance directives and portable medical orders in FHIR, including scanned PDFs. A [September 2026 Altarum analysis](https://altarum.org/news-and-insights/why-america-still-cant-honor-end-life-wishes-closing-polst-gap) finds that state POLST registries still lack common standards for submission, updating and retrieval. It says a registry works only with stable funding, clear ownership and workflows that EMS actually use.
- Two trends stand out:
  - HIE-based sharing is replacing standalone registries. Examples are Arizona (Contexture), DC and Maryland (CRISP), North Dakota (NDHIN), West Virginia (WVHIN), Maryland's October 2025 hospital feed, and the New York SHIN-NY's interest in eMOLST.
  - Standalone registries keep closing when funding or a vendor goes away: Washington, Louisiana's LaPOST eRegistry, Michigan, Rhode Island, and possibly Kentucky.

## How Deathbot could use this

- Copies come first. Registries are a supplement, never a substitute. The documents that matter in a crisis are the ones the proxy holds, the ones in the doctor's chart, and, for a POLST or EMS DNR, the original posted where responders will look. The agent should always cover these steps before mentioning any registry.
- In a state with an active self-file registry, mention it once as an optional extra step. Tell the person what it costs, how to file (online or by mail), and who can see the document. Many registries are not checked by EMS, and some are not checked by hospitals either. Say so plainly, and suggest the person give the registration card or number to their proxy.
- In a state without one, say there is no state registry. Do not invent one, and do not steer the person to a paid private vault as if it were required. Where a state points residents to a specific service, the agent can mention it neutrally.
- POLST registries are mostly clinician-run. A person cannot file a POLST themselves in most states. The useful step is to ask the clinician who fills out the POLST to enter it in the registry, if the state has one.
- Store this as a dated fact card per jurisdiction, separate from the forms guide. Fields: registry name, status, how to file, cost, who can see it, and last-checked date. The forms guide can then point to the card. The card should say "as of <date>" when the agent uses it, and the agent should hedge if the card is more than about a year old.
- Recheck every 6 to 12 months, and sooner for entries marked unclear or with a known date pending. Illinois and Massachusetts both have registries due in 2027, California's eRegistry is still in development, and California's SB 1088 takes effect Jan 1, 2027. The agent should never send people to a registry whose site has gone dark, such as Michigan's mipeaceofmind.org.
