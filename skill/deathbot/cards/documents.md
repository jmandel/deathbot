# The documents: living will, health care proxy, DNR order, POLST

Last verified: 2026-11-13

## In one breath
A living will and a health care proxy are legal documents you sign yourself; they guide later decisions but are not medical orders. DNR orders and POLST forms are medical orders signed by a clinician; National POLST says these, not advance directives, are what emergency crews can follow. All are voluntary.

## What it is and what it involves
(Layer 1. General US picture; state-specific handling lives elsewhere in the skill.)

**Living will (instruction directive).** A document you sign giving directions about care when you are permanently unconscious or death is imminent; much decision-making happens before that point [3]. It is used only if you are not able to make decisions about your care [29].

**Health care proxy (durable power of attorney for health care; the person is your agent, proxy or surrogate).** A document naming someone to make decisions about your health if you can't [29]. You can give that person a wide range of decisions or only a few, name an alternate, and change your proxy at any time [4].

**No proxy named.** State law decides who speaks for you. A review of statutes in force on 31 March 2016 found 41 of 51 jurisdictions (50 states plus DC) had a default-surrogate provision; the ranked lists always put spouse, child and parent among the top classes [5]. The count is historical. An unmarried partner can be left out [1].

**In-hospital DNR (DNAR) order.** A clinician's order, written after talking with you, your proxy or your family, not to attempt CPR if your heart or breathing stops. It covers CPR only, not other treatment such as medicines or nutrition [6].

**Out-of-hospital DNR.** A state form, wallet card or bracelet telling emergency crews not to attempt resuscitation outside a hospital [1][6].

**POLST (called MOLST, POST, MOST or LST in some states) [8].** A portable medical order for people with serious progressive illness or advanced frailty. It records whether to attempt CPR, a treatment level (full, selective or comfort-focused) and feeding-tube choices [9][10]. A clinician must sign it (which clinicians varies by state); National POLST says most states also require your or your surrogate's signature [8][10]. It takes effect when signed; a legal surrogate can complete one for a person who lacks capacity; each state adopts its own form [10]. It cannot appoint a proxy [11]. National POLST says healthy people do not need one [9].

**Signing, cost, choice.** States vary in their requirements for witnesses and notarization [12]. NIA says a lawyer is not required [1]. Medicare Part B covers advance care planning at no charge within the "Welcome to Medicare" or yearly wellness visit if the clinician accepts assignment; within other treatment, the deductible and 20% coinsurance apply [29]. Hospitals, nursing facilities, home health agencies and hospices in Medicare may not make care conditional on whether you have a directive [26].

**Changing your mind.** You can update an advance directive at any time [29], have a DNR order withdrawn [6], or void a POLST (tell your clinician; changes need a new signed form) [13]. Keep documents consistent with each other and replace old copies [1][13].

**Crossing state lines.** CaringInfo says one state's directive does not always work in another: some states honour it, some only if it resembles their own law, and some have no answer [12]. National POLST says a POLST will "most likely" be honoured elsewhere, with limits [13].

## What it is like
For the person: forms, a conversation with whoever you name, and often witnesses. A POLST is completed with a clinician after discussing diagnosis and prognosis [8]. The original stays with you, somewhere crews can find it [8][13].

For the family: a systematic review (40 studies, 2,854 surrogates, search to July 2010) reported that its quantitative studies found at least one third of surrogates had negative emotional effects such as stress, guilt and doubt, often lasting months and not always separable from grief. Knowing the person's wishes was often cited as easing it [16].

## What changes the odds
- **Order versus directive.** National POLST says emergency responders who find a person unconscious at home without clear instructions must try everything possible, such as CPR, a breathing tube or rapid transport [9], and that advance directives "are not orders that EMS providers can follow" [7][11].
- **Being found.** National POLST's comparison chart says an advance directive is "not always" easily found [11]. Hospitals and the other provider types above must record whether you have a directive [26]; that is not the same as holding a copy (reasoning, no source).
- **A provider may decline.** NIA says a provider may refuse a directive that goes against conscience, the institution's policy or accepted standards, and must then tell your proxy and consider transferring your care [1]. The federal rule allows a conscience refusal only where state law does [26].
- **Hospital admission.** National POLST guidance says a POLST should be honoured but reviewed against the person's current condition [10].

## The other paths
National POLST guidance sets out these levels [10]; state forms differ. On the national model, choosing CPR requires full treatment, so selective and comfort-focused orders go with no CPR.

- **Full treatment with CPR.** All means to maintain life, including intensive care, a breathing tube and a ventilator. This is also the default in an emergency when no order exists [9]. Burden: National POLST says these treatments can be painful and are often ineffective for people already near the end of life [9]. See the CPR and ventilation cards.
- **No CPR, otherwise full treatment.** Hospital and intensive care, including a breathing tube before an arrest, but no resuscitation attempt. If the heart stops, you die (reasoning, no source).
- **Selective treatment.** Hospital care for treatable conditions (IV fluids, antibiotics, heart monitoring); no breathing tube or ventilator; intensive care avoided if possible. Burden: you may die of an illness a ventilator could have carried you through (reasoning, no source).
- **Comfort-focused treatment.** Medicines and other measures for pain and breathlessness, usually where you live; hospital only if comfort cannot be achieved there. It "does not mean no care". Hospital care may still be used to restore comfort. Burden: some life-prolonging treatment is forgone, and you may die sooner (reasoning, no source).
- **Time-limited trial.** A full-treatment order can carry a written limit, such as a breathing tube for up to two weeks, then comfort-focused care unless improvement is clear. That is an illustration, not a standard length.
- **No documents, or only talking.** Full treatment in an emergency [9]; later decisions fall to a default surrogate where state law names one [1][5]. NIA calls conversation the most important part of planning [1].
- **Proxy only, or living will only.** A proxy covers unforeseen situations [1] but rests on one person's judgement under strain [16][20]. A living will leaves others to interpret it (reasoning, no source).

## Applying this to one person
(Layer 3.) Fair to reason about, conditionally: which documents fit this person. Who decides turns on decision-making capacity, not on whether the person can speak; how capacity is determined is not covered here. Facts that settle it: their state; whether a clinician regards them as seriously ill or frail (a POLST is the clinician's order to sign); what already exists, whether the documents agree and who holds copies; whether the proxy knows their wishes. No validated tool was found that predicts whether a person's documents will be found or followed: give no personal odds and no guarantees. Do not use the 2016 statute count to say what a state does now.

## Common misunderstandings
- "My living will stops paramedics doing CPR." National POLST says emergency responders cannot use an advance directive; they follow medical orders [7][11].
- "DNR means no treatment." It covers CPR only [6].
- "My family will know what I want." In hypothetical scenarios, surrogates matched the patient's answer 68% of the time [20].
- "A POLST is for everyone, and always means less treatment." It is meant for the seriously ill or frail, and it can order full treatment [9][10].
