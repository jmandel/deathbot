# Comfort-focused care and hospice

Last verified: 2026-11-13

## In one breath
Comfort-focused care has one goal: relieving symptoms (pain, breathlessness, fear), not curing the illness or extending life. Hospice is how Medicare pays for that care for people expected to live six months or less. At home it is a visiting service: staff come a few times a week, and family or hired help do the rest.

## What it is and what it involves
**Palliative care** aims at quality of life in serious illness; it "can be given with or without curative care" and may itself use chemotherapy, radiation or surgery when these relieve a symptom [2]. **Hospice** is palliative care for the last months, with attempts to cure the illness stopped [1].

**Medicare hospice benefit** [3][4][5][6][7]:
- Eligibility: Medicare Part A; the hospice doctor and the person's regular doctor (if any) certify a life expectancy of six months or less if the illness runs its normal course; the person, or an authorized representative if the person is incapacitated, signs a statement choosing hospice instead of other Medicare treatment for the terminal illness and related conditions.
- Duration: two 90-day periods, then unlimited 60-day periods, each recertified by a hospice doctor.
- Covered: nurse, doctor, aide, social worker and counselor visits; symptom drugs; equipment and supplies; grief support for family; a nurse and doctor on call (not present) 24 hours a day. Cost: up to $5 per outpatient prescription for pain and symptoms; 5% of the Medicare-approved amount for respite stays, capped at the inpatient deductible.
- Not covered: treatment intended to cure the terminal illness or related conditions. Room and board, except during short inpatient or respite stays the hospice arranges. Emergency room, hospital or ambulance care that the hospice did not arrange, unless it is unrelated to the terminal illness and related conditions: the person may owe the whole bill.
- Unrelated conditions stay covered by Medicare, with the usual deductibles and coinsurance. The person can ask the hospice for a list of what it considers unrelated.
- Four levels: routine home care (98.8% of hospice days, 2024); continuous home care (mostly nursing, at least 8 hours a day, brief crises only); general inpatient care (symptoms unmanageable elsewhere); inpatient respite (up to 5 days at a time, to rest the caregiver).
- Leaving: the person can stop ("revoke") at any time, return to their earlier Medicare coverage (Medicare Advantage members can stay in their plan throughout if they keep paying its premiums), and re-enroll later if eligible. The person can change hospice once in each benefit period. A hospice can discharge someone it judges no longer terminally ill; the person can request a fast review.

## What it is like
**For the person.** In the last days most people sleep more, stop eating and drinking, and lose the ability to talk. Hands and feet cool. Breathing becomes irregular, with pauses. Some have noisy, wet breathing (the "death rattle"), which in most cases does not upset the dying person. Going without food or water is generally not painful. Not everyone has pain [8].

Treatment: morphine or similar drugs for pain and breathlessness, a fan, a raised head of the bed; side effects include drowsiness, confusion and hallucinations [8]. Noisy breathing: turning the person, sometimes drugs [8]. Agitation or delirium that nothing else relieves: sedatives, sometimes to unconsciousness ("palliative sedation") [9]. Treatment does not always work: among 203 cancer patients dying in specialist palliative units in the US and Brazil (2010 to 2011), breathlessness worsened toward death and the share able to communicate fell from 80% to 39% in the last week [10].

**Without a ventilator.** Among ICU patients who died after a breathing machine was removed, death usually came within hours (table). **Without CPR.** When the heart stops, no chest compressions, shocks or breathing tube are used; comfort care continues and the person dies (reasoning, no source). See the documents card.

**For the family at home.** "The day-to-day care of a person dying at home is provided by family and friends" [1]: giving medicines including morphine, turning, cleaning, feeding, watching at night, deciding when to call the hospice.

Not captured: the hospice comparisons are not randomized and families answer after the death, so they do not show cause. Survival evidence is specific to each intervention. Palliative care added to treatment: one randomized trial (151 people, metastatic non-small-cell lung cancer) found longer median survival (11.6 vs 8.9 months, P=0.02) [15]; seven pooled trials (2,184 patients, inconsistent results) found no association (hazard ratio 0.90, 95% CI 0.69 to 1.17) [16]. Hospice enrollment: in claims for 4,493 Medicare patients with heart failure or five cancers (1998 to 2002), mean survival was 29 days longer with hospice; not randomized, and from the hospice trade association [17]. None of this covers stopping a treatment that is keeping someone alive, such as dialysis or a ventilator; stopping that shortens life (reasoning, no source).

## What changes the odds
- **Time enrolled.** Cancer patients' families reported the best outcomes after more than 30 days of hospice [12].
- **Which hospice.** Of 3,107 hospices (2017 to 2019), 31.1% of for-profit and 12.5% of not-for-profit ones scored 3 or more points below the national average on family-reported experience; 21.9% and 33.7% scored 3 or more above [19].
- **A caregiver at home.** In a 2008 to 2009 survey of 591 hospices, 12% required one [20].

## The other paths
- **Continuing disease treatment, with palliative care alongside.** Keeps the chance of more time; pooled trials show a modest quality-of-life gain from the palliative care [16]. The burdens are the treatment's own: side effects, clinic and hospital time (reasoning, no source).
- **A time-limited trial.** Treatment continues until a date agreed in advance, then is reviewed against agreed signs of benefit; the treatment's burdens continue meanwhile (reasoning, no source; see the ventilation card).
- **Comfort-focused care without enrolling in hospice.** Palliative care teams work in hospitals, clinics, nursing homes and homes; Medicare coverage "depends on your benefits and treatment plan" [1].
- **Dying in hospital or ICU.** Care there can also be comfort-focused. Staff are always present, the family does not do the nursing, and a reversible problem can be treated if that is the plan (reasoning, no source). Among 1,146 families of Medicare patients who died of lung or colorectal cancer by 2011, 42.2% rated care excellent after a hospital death vs 57.4% otherwise [21]; an association, not proof of cause.
- **Comfort care in a nursing home, with hospice visiting.** Room and board is not paid by the hospice benefit (median $315 a day for a shared room, 2025 [22]). If the resident is eligible for Medicaid and Medicaid is paying the facility, the state Medicaid agency pays it through the hospice [7].
- **No caregiver at home.** Hired non-medical help (median $35 an hour, 2025 [22]), a facility, or a hospice that does not require a caregiver [20].

## Applying this to one person
Fair to reason about: who would do the hands-on care, nights included, and for how long; whether a current treatment would have to stop (ask the hospice); whether the person qualifies (only doctors certify); who would sign if the person lacks capacity.

Information that helps planning (none of it settles how long someone has): the doctor's estimate of time and how sure they are; local hospices' Care Compare scores, after-hours response and inpatient unit; whether the person has Medicaid.

Nothing on this card can predict for an individual how long hospice will last or how comfortable death will be. Give no personal estimate.

## Common misunderstandings
- "Hospice is a place." Usually it is a team visiting wherever the person lives, most often a private home [5].
- "Someone from hospice stays around the clock." Routine care averages about four visits a week [5].
- "Sedation at the end is what kills." Non-randomized studies, mostly of cancer patients, found no statistically significant survival difference with sedation; the evidence is low quality [9][18].
- "Hospice guarantees a comfortable death." At the median hospice, 1 in 10 families said needed symptom help came only sometimes or never [5].
