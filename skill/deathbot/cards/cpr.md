# CPR (cardiopulmonary resuscitation)

Last verified: 2026-11-13

## In one breath
CPR is the emergency attempt to restart a heart that has stopped: forceful chest compressions and breaths, with electric shocks when the heart rhythm allows, drugs, and often a breathing tube. In US registries about 1 in 10 adults leave hospital alive after CPR for an arrest outside hospital and about 2 in 10 after one in hospital; the odds are lower with advanced age, frailty or serious illness. A cardiac arrest is fatal unless treatment begins immediately.

## What it is and what it involves
*Layer 1: established fact.*

- **Cardiac arrest** means the heart suddenly stops pumping. The person collapses, loses consciousness and stops breathing normally [1].
- **Compressions:** someone repeatedly pushes on the chest with force while air is put into the lungs. The force can break ribs [2, 3].
- **Shocks (defibrillation)** help only in a "shockable" rhythm. Most arrests do not start in one: 81.3% of adult out-of-hospital arrests (CARES, 2025) and 81% of in-hospital arrests were non-shockable [4, 5].
- **Drugs and airway:** of 348,996 adults given CPR in US hospitals (2000-2021), 50.0% had a breathing tube placed during the arrest, and 21.4% were already on a ventilator [6].
- **How long:** in that registry, hearts that restarted did so after a median 7 minutes (middle half 3-13); when they did not, teams stopped after a median 20 minutes (14-30) [6].
- **Afterward:** if the heart restarts, treatment continues with support for blood pressure and breathing while the cause is looked for [5].
- **CPR is generally the default:** the National Institute on Aging says that without a DNR order, medical staff will try to restore breathing and heart rhythm [7].

## What it is like
**For the person:** they are unconscious when CPR starts [1]. Physical, cognitive and emotional effects can last months or years; recovery can involve rehabilitation and caregivers [8].

**For the family:** in a French randomized trial of 570 relatives (arrests outside hospital), relatives in the usual-practice group (where 43% watched CPR anyway) had more post-traumatic stress symptoms at 90 days than those routinely offered the chance to watch (79% watched); odds ratio 1.7, 95% CI 1.2-2.5 [9]. If the heart restarts, a DNR order often follows: among hospital patients 65 and older whose hearts restarted (2000-2008), 48% to 56% across four subgroups had one written afterward, a median 10 hours later. The paper gives the middle half as 2-70 hours in its abstract and 2-72 in its text [10].

## What changes the odds
- **Rhythm:** outside hospital, 28.9% survive a shockable rhythm and 6.2% a non-shockable one [4].
- **Being seen, and where:** unwitnessed 4.3%; witnessed by a bystander 15.9%; in a public place 20.8%; at home 8.9% [4].
- **Age and prior function:** see table. Dependence in daily activities stayed associated with lower survival after adjustment, compared with independent people living at home [10].
- **How survivors leave hospital:** among survivors 65 and older, one-year survival was 72.8% with mild or no neurologic disability, 61.1% moderate, 42.2% severe, 10.2% in coma or vegetative state. At three years their survival (43.5%) matched that of patients discharged after heart failure (44.9%) [18].
- **Survivors of arrest outside hospital, 65+:** of 1,127 discharged alive (CARES 2005-2010), 52.6% went home and 31.8% died within a year [20].

## The other paths
**No CPR (a DNR order).** Before any arrest, nothing else changes: a DNR "is specific about CPR. It does not have instructions for other treatments, such as pain medicine, other medicines, or nutrition" [21]. When the heart stops, nobody starts compressions, the person loses consciousness, and death follows, often within minutes [1, 22]. No resuscitation is attempted; other care, including hospital treatment if wanted, continues. Burden: death at that moment is close to certain, including in arrests that might have been survived (reasoning, no source).

**Comfort-focused care.** A DNR may be part of a hospice plan, where the aim is treating symptoms such as pain or shortness of breath, not prolonging life [21]. A DNR alone is not a comfort plan; see the comfort-care-and-hospice card.

**CPR, then reassess.** A person can want CPR attempted and tell their proxy and clinicians in advance what outcomes would be unacceptable; after a restart, others may have to decide for them. Burdens: uncertainty in intensive care and a heavy decision for the proxy. How and when doctors judge the outlook after a restart is a question for the treating team (all reasoning, no source).

**Not being intubated (DNI)** is a separate order [7]. A search on 2026-11-13 found no outcome data for partial orders; do not call them a gentler CPR.

**Making it hold.** In hospital the provider writes the DNR in the medical record. For home and other non-hospital settings, the provider can explain how to get a wallet card, bracelet or other DNR documents so emergency crews can recognize the order; standard forms may come from the state health department [21]. Rules differ by place: check locally. If the person cannot make their own medical decisions, a named health care agent or legal guardian can agree to a DNR; without one, a family member can in some circumstances; family may not override a DNR the person requested [21]. A person who changes their mind should tell the provider and family right away and destroy documents that include the DNR [21]. See the documents card.

## Applying this to one person
*Layer 3 guidance.*

Fair conditional reasoning: say which row fits the person's setting and that it describes a group. Do not carry an in-hospital figure over to an arrest at home or the reverse, treat a subgroup comparison as a prediction for one person, combine rows into a personal percentage, or present any figure or score as a guarantee.

Facts that inform the discussion: in hospital or at home? Independent in daily activities? Cancer stage? On dialysis? In a nursing home? What outcome would the person count as worth it?

**GO-FAR score** [23]. A point score for in-hospital arrest only, built from age and pre-arrest conditions in US registry data from 2007-2009. It predicts survival to discharge with at most minimal deficits (CPC 1), a stricter bar than the table's CPC 1-2. Pooled external validations gave a C statistic of 0.78 (0.69-0.85) [24]. Limits: old data; two published corrections to the original paper could not be read, so its item list and group results are left out here; later single-hospital validations found it predicted death in some patients who did well; and ILCOR's 2025 evidence update found still no evidence to support using any prediction model for DNR decisions [25]. A hospital clinician can calculate and discuss it; do not compute or quote a personal result.

## Common misunderstandings
- **"CPR usually works."** Of 500 people surveyed in a California emergency department in 2016, at least half put success above 75% in every scenario [26]. Real figures are in the table.
- **"If it works, you are back to normal."** About half of survivors 65 and older left hospital with more than mild neurologic disability, and over half went to a nursing or rehab facility (table) [18].
- **"CPR never works for old or sick people."** Survival is lower, not zero (table).
- **"DNR means no treatment," or "my living will covers it."** A DNR is an order about CPR only [21]; a living will is not a medical order [7].

- **Arrest outside hospital, by older age band or by illness** (cancer, dialysis, frailty): the CARES report separates only children from adults; the illness rows in the table are in-hospital only.
- **Whether newer studies supersede the 2000-2012 older-adult cohorts:** searched, none found; not exhaustive. The AHA 2026 statistics chapter on cardiac arrest could not be opened (403).
