# Mechanical ventilation (breathing machines), short and prolonged

Last verified: 2026-11-13

## In one breath
A ventilator pushes air into the lungs through a tube in the windpipe while the underlying illness is treated; it does not fix that illness. Some people need it for hours or days and recover. In the studies below, many older adults who needed one died within a year; in groups on prolonged ventilation, more than half died within a year, and in one US centre few survivors were fully independent.

## What it is and what it involves
(Sources 1, 3, 4 unless marked.)
- **Intubation.** After medicine to make the person sleepy, a clinician passes a plastic tube through the mouth into the windpipe and connects it to the machine, usually in an intensive care unit (ICU).
- **While the tube is in.** The person cannot talk or eat; food goes through a tube to the stomach or into a vein. Staff suction mucus through the tube, which causes coughing. Sedatives can make the person very sleepy. Some people are restrained so tubes are not pulled out.
- **Coming off ("weaning").** The tube is removed once the person can breathe alone: hours or days for some, longer for others. Some never can.
- **Tracheostomy.** For long ventilation a surgeon puts a shorter tube through a hole in the front of the neck (median day 12 for people 65+ in source 9). It may allow talking with a special valve and some eating, and can be removed if no longer needed.
- **Long-term ventilation.** Some people live on a ventilator in a facility; some go home if stable, with caregivers trained to run the machine, change the tracheostomy tube, suction mucus and respond to alarms (source 5).
- **Risks.** Pneumonia, collapsed lung, vocal cord damage, blood clots, bedsores, weak breathing muscles (source 2).

## What it is like
**For the person.** Of 150 survivors of 48+ hours of ventilation at one US centre, two thirds remembered the tube or the ICU; pain, fear and being unable to speak were among what bothered them (source 6, 2002). A patient guide says about 7 in 10 people get delirium (severe, often frightening confusion) on a breathing machine or soon after, citing no study (source 7).

**For the family.** A person with decision-making capacity keeps the right to decide, even with a tube in; without capacity, a surrogate decides, guided by the person's known wishes or, failing that, their best interests (source 31; ethics guidance, not state law). In one US study of 126 people on prolonged ventilation, the 103 who left hospital alive changed care setting a median of 4 times in the year (source 8).

## What changes the odds
- **Age, frailty and other illness.** At 80+, recovery was less likely with older age, frailty, more illnesses and greater severity (source 14).
- **Severe COPD.** Of 4,791 Medicare patients on home oxygen intubated for a flare in non-rural ICUs in 2008 (mean age 74), 23.3% died in hospital, a further 45.2% of the original group died in the 12 months after discharge, and 31.5% of the original group were alive a year after discharge (source 18, 2015).
- **Advanced dementia.** Among ventilated nursing home residents with advanced dementia, 51.1% died in hospital or within 30 days of admission (2012). For 1-year deaths the paper's text says "consistently greater than 80%"; its table for two subsets of hospitals shows 67.5% and 70.0%; these differ (source 19, 2016).

## The other paths
**Mask ventilation.** A tight mask pushes air in; the person stays awake. In COPD flares with carbon dioxide build-up and acid blood, trials of two-level mask ventilation (BiPAP) added to usual care lowered hospital deaths (risk ratio 0.54, 0.38-0.76; moderate-quality evidence; source 20, 2017). In heart-failure fluid on the lungs not needing immediate intubation, trials of CPAP or BiPAP suggest it may lower hospital deaths (risk ratio 0.65, 0.51-0.82; low-quality evidence; source 21, 2019). In 27 studies of 2,020 people with do-not-intubate orders who got mask ventilation, 56% (49-64) left hospital alive and 32% (21-45) were alive at 1 year; hospital survival was 68% in COPD, 68% in heart-failure fluid, 41% in pneumonia, 37% in cancer (source 22, 2018). Burden in the COPD trials: 11 more people in 100 (4 to 17) could not tolerate it than usual care (source 20).

**High-flow nasal oxygen.** In a Brazilian trial (1,766 people, mean age 64, 2019-2023) intubation or death within 7 days occurred in 39% with high flow and 38% with mask ventilation; the authors call the results uncertain for COPD, heart-failure fluid and people with weakened immunity (source 23, 2025).

**Time-limited trial.** An agreed plan to use the ventilator for a set period, then continue, extend, or change to comfort care depending on the response. The American Thoracic Society says evidence of its effects is thin and that done badly it may cause harm (source 24, 2024).

**Stopping a ventilator already running.** Ethics guidance sees no ethical difference between not starting and stopping (source 31). Comfort medicines are given first; the tube is removed in one step, or support is turned down over as little as 5 to 60 minutes. Gasping or noisy breathing can occur and is treated; freedom from distress cannot be promised (sources 25, 26). Among 1,505 adults who died after withdrawal in 14 Washington State hospitals (2003-2008), median time to death was 0.93 hours (middle half 0.25 to 5.5 hours). That study counted only people who died; some people live on after withdrawal (sources 26, 27).

**Not intubating.** Other treatment, including mask ventilation, can continue, and some people recover. What a person in respiratory failure feels varies with the cause: breathlessness or air hunger, sometimes confusion or drowsiness; some become extremely sleepy or lose consciousness (source 28). If the failure is not reversed the person dies; no source for how long that takes was found. Breathlessness near death is treated with small doses of opioids such as morphine, sitting upright, a fan, and sometimes oxygen or anxiety medicine (source 29). Opioid trials in advanced illness, reviewed to 2015 and not specific to the last days of life, show some benefit (low-quality evidence), with drowsiness, nausea and constipation (source 30). Relief can mean being sleepier, and at home family usually give the medicines (reasoning, no source; see the comfort-care card).

**Paying for what follows.** Medicare does not pay for long-term (custodial) care in a nursing home or at home; it treats skilled nursing facility care separately, and Medicaid may cover long-term care for those who meet their state's rules (source 32). Medicare Part A does cover care in a long-term care hospital (source 33).

## Applying this to one person
- Conditional reasoning is fair; a personal probability is not. Say "in a 2008 Medicare group intubated for a COPD flare while on home oxygen, about 3 in 10 were alive a year after discharge", then how this person differs.
- Facts that settle the picture: likely reason for ventilation; current function and frailty; lung function and home oxygen; cancer or dementia stage; whether mask ventilation was tolerated before.
- A short trial, weeks on a ventilator and a tracheostomy are separate decisions. Record the person's exact wording.
- Disability does not by itself say how a person will judge their life: for the 70 one-year survivors in source 8, surrogates' questionnaire answers put quality of life as good for 27% and poor for 49%; survivors' own ratings were not reported. Ask what they would find acceptable.
- **ProVent 14** (source 17): only for someone already ventilated 14 days; scored by the ICU team from age, platelet count, need for blood-pressure drugs or dialysis, and trauma or not. Age 65+ scores 2; non-trauma adds 1. Observed 1-year deaths in its two groups (n=491 and n=245): score 3, 61% (52-70) and 81% (71-90); score 4-6, 92% (84-100) and 88% (75-100). **ProVent** (source 16) is the day-21 version. Limits: US academic centres, mean age 54; predict death, not function; not meant to decide treatment alone; for advance planning they only illustrate.
- This card's search (November 2026) found no validated tool that predicts beforehand how one person would do; that is not proof none exists.

## Common misunderstandings
- "Once on, you can't come off." A capable patient can ask for it to be stopped; so can a surrogate acting on the wishes or best interests of a patient who lacks capacity (source 31).
- "Refusing intubation means refusing treatment." Other treatment, including mask ventilation, can continue.
